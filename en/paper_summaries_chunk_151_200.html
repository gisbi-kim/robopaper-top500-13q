Robert J. Wood
https://doi.org/10.1109/tro.2008.916997
This paper, which is the starting point of the RoboBee series, is a hardware milestone that shows that a 60 mg-class flapping-wing microrobot has reached a level where it can take off with its own wing thrust.
01
Background
Insect-sized flying robots are a field that simultaneously requires ultra-small aerodynamic, driving, and manufacturing technologies in disaster search, environmental monitoring, and biomimetic flight research.
02
Problem
The paper addresses the challenge of creating a lift sufficient to overcome the weight by integrating the wing flapping structure, actuator, and transmission at the actual insect scale.
03
Prior limitations
Existing micro aerial vehicles failed to secure sufficient power density and precision manufacturing at the insect scale, so they often remained in tethered thrust tests or partial modules.
04
Goal
The goal is to experimentally demonstrate that a biologically inspired at-scale robotic insect can achieve first takeoff.
05
Method
The method is to fabricate a piezoelectric actuator, flexure-based transmission, flapping wing mechanism, and ultra-light airframe and measure thrust under tethered takeoff conditions.
06
Core idea
The core idea is to reproduce the necessary wing movement using ultra-small fabrication and compliant mechanisms rather than replicating the abnormal aerodynamic force of insect wings themselves.
07
Validation
Verification is a hardware experiment conducted through flapping kinematics, thrust, and takeoff demonstration of an actually produced60mg class microrobot.
08
Results
The result was that the robot generated an average lift exceeding its own weight and exhibited first takeoff, not including precise postural stabilization.
09
Comparison
From a comparative perspective, an insect scale that is much smaller than existing small flight platforms has been achieved, but free flight control and onboard power still remain separate tasks.
10
Significance
The significance is that it raised the production of micro-robotic insects from a concept to a system that produces actual aerodynamic performance and laid the foundation for future RoboBee research.
11
Limitations
Limitations include dependence on tether and external power/control, short flight stability, lack of payload, and fabrication yield issues that were not fully resolved at the paper stage.
12
Future work
Future challenges include expansion to onboard power, closed-loop attitude control, autonomous sensing, mass manufacturing, and group operation.
