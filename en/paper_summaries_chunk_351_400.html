FCL: A general purpose library for collision and proximity queries

Jia Pan; Sachin Chitta; Dinesh Manocha
https://doi.org/10.1109/icra.2012.6225337

FCL is a paper that reduces the geometric calculation bottleneck of real-time motion planning by combining repeated collision and distance queries in robot planning and simulation into a general-purpose C++ library.

01
Background
Robotic motion planning, grasping, manipulation, and simulation rely on geometry-based infrastructure that must quickly calculate collisions and minimum distances between numerous objects and links.
02
Problem
The paper addresses the problem of processing collision detection and proximity queries with a consistent API for various convex/non-convex meshes and primitives.
03
Prior limitations
Existing collision libraries are tied to specific shapes, specific planners, or research codes, making it difficult to ensure both reusability and performance in robot software.
04
Goal
The goal is to provide a general-purpose collision/proximity library that the motion planning community can use as is and to create a foundation that can be connected to the OMPL·MoveIt series.
05
Method
The method is to implement bounding volume hierarchy, mesh/primitive support, distance query, continuous collision checking, and broad/narrow phase structure with C++ API.
06
Core idea
The core idea is to separate the collision calculation into a standard module independent of the planner, rather than a single algorithm, so that the same query grammar can be used in multiple robots, environments, and geometric expressions.
07
Validation
Verification is centered on library evaluation that shows query time and functional range in various shapes and robot planning cases, and is more in the nature of a software benchmark than an actual robot field experiment.
08
Results
The results showed that FCL was fast and flexible enough to integrate collision/proximity queries into the motion planning stack, and was later widely used as MoveIt's core collision backend.
09
Comparison
The target of comparison is the existing collision library series such as PQP, RAPID, and ODE, and FCL is strong in that it includes distance and continuous collision queries required for robot planning.
10
Significance
The significance is that motion planning research focuses on the algorithm itself on a standardized geometry backend rather than reimplementing collision detection every time.
11
Limitations
The limitation is that since it is a library paper, it is not a paper that directly increases the success rate of a specific planner, and deformable objects and learned geometry representation are outside the scope of the paper.
12
Future work
Future challenges naturally extend to the combination of GPU parallelization, dynamic scene updates, uncertain geometry, and differentiable collision costs.
13

The curvature-velocity method for local obstacle avoidance

Reid G. Simmons
https://doi.org/10.1109/robot.1996.511023

The Curvature-Velocity Method is a classic paper that performs local obstacle avoidance in real time by incorporating the dynamic constraints of a mobile robot into velocity space exploration.

01
Background
Indoor mobile robots must immediately avoid obstacles while moving in the target direction within incomplete sensor information and limited driving capabilities.
02
Problem
The paper solves the local navigation problem of selecting a safe and goal-oriented command among the robot's possible combinations of curvature and velocity.
03
Prior limitations
Existing potential-field or simple reactive avoidance did not sufficiently reflect the robot's speed, acceleration, and curvature constraints, which could create a risk of vibration or collision in the actual moving body.
04
Goal
The goal is to enable a nonholonomic mobile robot to select collision-free motion within a local control space that can be calculated in real time.
05
Method
The method calculates the allowable curvature-velocity pairs from obstacles and selects the final command with an objective that evaluates target progress, speed, and safety distance.
06
Core idea
The key idea is to simultaneously evaluate safety and efficiency in the velocity command space that the actual robot can immediately execute, rather than creating the path in space first.
07
Validation
Verification is performed using indoor mobile robot driving and obstacle avoidance examples of a simulation nature, and large-scale public dataset evaluation does not exist at the time.
08
Results
The results showed that CVM can quickly avoid obstacles and advance to the goal even with limited computational resources, and has since been frequently cited along with the Dynamic Window series of local planners.
09
Comparison
The comparison is that with potential-field type reactive control, CVM provides more feasible local avoidance in that it is dynamics-aware command selection.
10
Significance
The significance is to create a trend that local planners must explicitly consider kinematics and dynamics constraints in mobile robot navigation.
11
Limitations
The limitation is that since it is a local method, it does not guarantee global optimality and complex dead-end escape, and sensor noise and dynamic obstacle processing are also limited.
12
Future work
Future tasks include integration with global planner, dynamic obstacle prediction, uncertainty-based cost, and ROS navigation stack implementation.
